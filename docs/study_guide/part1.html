How to use this guide

You said you know nothing about this project. This guide assumes exactly that. It starts with the problem in everyday words, then follows one video clip through the whole system, then opens each part of the machine one at a time. Every part has the same four things: a plain-English idea, an analogy, a diagram, and a box saying what to say when you present.

The one-minute version (memorise this first) Cameras record video all day, and nobody can watch it all. GenAI-VMS watches it for you. It cuts each camera's video into 10-second pieces, writes a small structured "report card" for every piece (who and what is in the scene, where, doing what), notices suspicious things with simple rules, asks a vision AI model "is that really happening?", links the same event across cameras, and then uses language AI to search the footage in plain words, explain incidents in phases with a written report that cites its evidence, and answer questions in a chat. Everything runs on a laptop with a 4 GB graphics card, using small models, and every AI answer says which model gave it and why.

1. The problem and the big idea

1.1 The problem

A video management system (VMS) is the software behind CCTV: it records cameras, shows them live, and lets you replay them. A campus or a shopping mall can have dozens of cameras recording 24 hours a day. That is thousands of hours of video, and almost all of it is boring. When something happens, a person has to scrub through hours of footage to find it.

Think of it as…A huge library where every book is a video tape and there is no catalogue. To find one scene you must open every tape. Our project hires a tireless librarian who watches every tape, writes a summary card for each, files the cards by meaning, and can also write you a short report on any incident — with footnotes pointing at the exact pages.

1.2 What we built, in three pillars

PillarThe question it answersThe idea
1. Multimodal search"Show me a person in a red top near the service door."Describe what you want in words (or with a picture). The system finds matching moments across all cameras, without you knowing the camera or the time.
2. Event reasoning"What actually happened, and how did it unfold?"Detect events with rules, confirm them with a vision AI, link them across cameras, and break an incident into five phases (before, building up, the incident, after).
3. Incident summarization"Write it up for me."An AI writes a structured incident report (and a PDF) in which every claim cites evidence. Plus a daily security report and a chat assistant.

1.3 Where the idea comes from (the research)

The project is based on three research papers, and you should be able to name them:

  1. De Silva et al., "LLMs for Video Surveillance Applications" (IEEE TENCON 2024) — turn video into text, then a language model can search and summarise it.
  2. Zhen et al., "MP-PVIR" (2025) — incident reasoning: split an incident into phases, describe each phase from each camera, then write the report with an AI, checking the format.
  3. Shen et al., "Reasoning Text-to-Video Retrieval via Digital Twins" (2025) — search by building a structured "digital twin" of each frame, then using a language model to reason over candidates.
Our own contributionThose papers each solve one pillar on short benchmark clips. We use one shared representation — the digital twin — to feed all three pillars, and we make it work on continuous live streams on small hardware, with a single operator-facing explanation per incident.

1.4 The constraints that shaped every decision

  • Hardware: each of us has a laptop with a 4 GB graphics card (RTX 3050). That is tiny for AI. So: small models (3 billion parameters), squeezed to 4 bits, and one heavy model at a time on the GPU.
  • Money: zero. Free tools only; cloud AI only through free tiers and only as an option.
  • Privacy: local-first. By default nothing leaves the laptop.
  • Time: a few weeks, two builders. So the work was split into two tracks (Divyansh and Jatin) that meet at strictly defined message formats ("contracts").
What to say"Surveillance generates more video than anyone can watch. We built a system that turns that video into structured knowledge, so operators can search it in plain language, get alerts that an AI has double-checked, and get incident reports that cite their evidence — all running locally on a 4 GB GPU laptop."

2.3 Seven design principles (they explain almost every "why")

  1. One representation, three pillars. The digital twin feeds search, event reasoning and summaries. Nobody re-watches raw video unless pixels are truly needed.
  2. Cheap filter first, expensive reasoning second. Rules before the vision AI; embeddings before the language-model rerank; correlation before phase reasoning.
  3. Metadata on Kafka, pixels in object storage. Messages carry links, never video.
  4. Local-first, cloud-switchable. Every AI call goes through one gateway chosen by task name and profile.
  5. Contract-first parallel work. Divyansh and Jatin build against agreed message formats, not each other's unfinished code.
  6. Evidence or it didn't happen. Every generated claim cites evidence ids.
  7. Degrade, don't die. If a model is down, features fall back; recording never stops.
What to say"If I remember one slogan: cheap filter first, expensive reasoning second. It is why a 4 GB laptop can run this."
