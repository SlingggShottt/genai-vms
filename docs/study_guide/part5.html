11. How to present it

11.1 The shape of a good talk

  1. Hook (problem): one person, many screens, hours of video.
  2. Idea: turn video into structured knowledge once; reuse it three ways.
  3. Show the machine: the big-picture figure (figure 2.1) and one clip's journey (figure 3.1).
  4. Show it working: the live demo.
  5. Show it is honest: results with their limits.
  6. Close: what you would do next.

11.2 The 1-minute pitch

Say this"Surveillance cameras record far more video than anyone can watch. Our system turns every ten-second clip into a structured report card — who and what is there, where, and doing what — and then uses that to do three things. First, you can search the footage in plain words. Second, it detects events with simple rules, has a vision AI double-check them, and links the same event across cameras. Third, it writes an incident report in phases, where every sentence cites its evidence, and you can chat with an assistant about it. It runs locally on a 4 GB GPU laptop, and we measured it honestly, including where it falls short."

11.5 Presentation habits that help

  • Tell the story of one clip rather than listing components.
  • Know five numbers by heart: 10 s segments; 4 GB GPU; 0.22 s fast search; ~15 s reason search; 966 MB perception memory.
  • Name a limit before they find it. "One thing I want to be clear about..."
  • When you do not know, say "I measured X; I did not measure Y". That is a strong answer.
  • Use analogies: post office (Kafka), warehouse / filing cabinet / meaning map (MinIO / Postgres / Qdrant), detective's report card (twin), one chair (GPU lease), footnotes (citations).

13. Questions you will be asked

Short, honest answers. Adapt them to your own voice.

1. Why not just send the video to a big cloud model?
Cost, privacy and volume. Hours of video from many cameras is far too large to send; the data is sensitive; and cloud models have rate limits. Our design converts video to small structured text once, locally, and uses cloud models only as an option for text reasoning (the hybrid profile).
2. What is a digital twin here?
A small structured JSON for each 10-second segment: objects, tracks, attributes, zones, motion and vectors. It is a text copy of what the video contains, so search, rules and reports read it instead of re-watching video.
3. Why Kafka?
It decouples fast and slow parts. The slow GPU service never blocks ingestion; a crashed service resumes from where it stopped; messages can be replayed. Messages carry links, not video.
4. How do you stop the AI making things up?
Structured outputs checked against a schema, retries that quote the errors, and mandatory citations: every claim must point to an evidence id that exists, and uncited claims are dropped. Also fixed tools for the assistant, numeric post-checks for the daily report, and provenance on every result.
5. Why both rules and an AI gate?
Rules are cheap and run on everything but produce false alarms; the vision model is accurate but slow and uses the GPU, so it only checks the suspicions. On real footage the gate rejected all 9 doorway "intrusions" and kept 30 of 30 loitering events.
6. How does it run on only 4 GB?
Small models (3B) squeezed to 4 bits, one model family on the GPU at a time through a lease, embeddings on the CPU, masks on the CPU on demand, and a "cheap before expensive" design. Perception needs under 1 GB.
7. What is the GPU lease?
A lock in Redis that says which model family currently occupies the GPU. Others wait their turn; it expires if the holder crashes (and after 15 minutes even if stuck).
8. What is the difference between fast and reason search?
Fast = picture and text similarity, about 0.2 s. Reason = a language model decomposes the query and re-reads the best candidates (and optionally a vision model checks details), about 15 s. Reason mode is the "reasoning-based" part of the title.
9. Did the fine-tuning work?
Technically yes: we trained a 3B vision model on a 4 GB GPU and served it. As an accuracy claim, not proven: the gain was only on labels made by a rule; on a person's labels there was no gain. We report that and we did not make it the default.
10. How do you know search works?
We built a benchmark from real MEVA labels (36 queries): fast mode finds a relevant clip in the top 10 for 20 of 36 queries (random would be about 9), reason mode 24. It is strong on objects and postures, weak on motion. The clips are staged, so it is a ceiling.
11. What does idempotent mean, and why do you care?
Doing an operation twice gives the same result as once. Kafka can redeliver messages, so every consumer writes idempotently to avoid duplicates after a restart.
12. How does correlation work without recognising people?
It uses time and a camera map: two events on connected cameras whose times fit are linked, and chains are merged with union-find. The limit is that it cannot tell whether it is the same person; re-identification is future work.
13. What happens if the language model is down?
Recording, live view, playback and fast search continue. Reason search falls back with a note, the assistant says the model is unavailable, the daily report uses a template, and an analysis job fails with a clear reason.
14. How is it secured?
Hashed passwords, short-lived JWTs, roles re-read from the database every call, a role-by-endpoint test matrix, rate limits, 15-minute presigned links, audit logging, no secrets in code. Face recognition is deliberately not built.
15. What is novel compared with the papers?
One shared digital twin feeding search, reasoning and reports; running on continuous live streams, not short clips; running on a 4 GB laptop; a unified operator experience with evidence-cited reports.
16. What did you personally build?
(Track D) ingestion, perception, the events service and the VLM gate, the LLM gateway and GPU lease, text search, reasoning and incident reports, the assistant, the deployment and the evaluations. Jatin built the API, frontend, correlation, alerts, indexing and the timeline. Several cross-track pieces were done deliberately and flagged.
17. Why Qdrant and Postgres?
Postgres for exact relational facts; Qdrant for similarity search with payload filters and hybrid dense+sparse search. Each does its job well.
18. Why JavaScript and not TypeScript?
A team decision based on skills. We compensate with runtime validation (zod) at the API boundary and tests.
19. How would you scale it?
Kafka partitions (6 per topic) let consumers scale out; the indexer can scale horizontally; heavy models would move to a bigger GPU or the cloud profile. The tested target here is 4 to 6 cameras.
20. What are the biggest limitations?
Small models, so quality is limited; search is weak on actions that need motion; the fine-tuning experiment was inconclusive because labels were mostly drafts; cloud and Kubernetes deployments were not exercised on real infrastructure; no user study.
21. What would you do next?
Collect more human-made labels and retest the adapter on them; train the second adapter; add person re-identification; finish the clean retrieval ablation; deploy to a VM and run the usability study.
22. What are MEVA and UCF-Crime?
Public research video datasets: MEVA has multi-camera everyday activity (we used bus-station cameras and activity clips); UCF-Crime has crime videos with labelled categories. We used them as stand-ins for live cameras.
23. What is MinIO / S3?
An object store: a place for big files, each with a link. S3 is Amazon's version; MinIO is a free one with the same interface, so the project can later switch to real S3.
24. How is the system tested if models are non-deterministic?
Unit tests never call a real model: a FakeGateway replays recorded answers; golden tests run the report writer on real stored evidence bundles; benchmarks measure the real models separately.
25. Why 10-second segments?
Short enough for low latency and cheap re-processing, long enough to track an object and keep file counts sane. It is a setting.
26. Is it legal / ethical to monitor people?
We use public research datasets and team members for demos, keep data local by default, avoid face recognition, log access, and expire footage. Real deployments need consent, signage and policy decisions that are not ours to make.
27. What is the "gate" rejecting?
Candidates where the vision model answers "no" (or "unsure" for a rule that does not accept it). Rejected ones are stored with their reason, never shown to operators, and useful for measuring the gate.
28. What does "provenance" mean?
The record of how a result was made: which model, how many tries, whether a fallback was used, how long each step took. It lets you audit and compare.
29. What if I ask for something that never happened?
Search returns the closest matches with scores (it cannot say "none exist" for a visual query, but the reason mode's trace and low scores show weak matches). The assistant says it found nothing for questions about records.
30. How long did it take, and how big is it?
About six weeks of two-person work (kickoff 17 September); roughly 74 stories planned, about three quarters finished and tested; thousands of automated tests across eight services, a shared library and a frontend.
